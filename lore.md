# JUNKMAIL — Lore

> **The important rule:** every new save is a slightly different universe.
>
> The differences are small enough that almost everybody believes their world is completely normal. The player is the problem: they have just arrived, they don't remember who they are, and they are the only person who notices that something is wrong.

---

## The common story

In every universe, **junk mail is normal**.

Every household receives packages containing random, mostly useless objects. People open them, keep what they want, sell what they don't, and complain about the occasional broken item. Nobody knows exactly why the system exists. Nobody seems particularly interested in finding out.

If you ask someone where the packages come from, they will usually give an answer that sounds reasonable but explains nothing:

> "The delivery companies send them. That's what delivery companies do."

The player has no such instinct.

At the beginning of a new save, the player knows almost nothing about themselves. They don't know their name. They don't remember moving into the house. They don't remember buying anything. They don't even recognize the house in the old photograph that is already among their possessions.

And yet everyone else acts as though the player has lived there forever.

The person the player is playing as **already existed**. You don't remember some things because **you are not that person**. That person used to be like everyone else, but **THEY ARE NOT YOU**.

### The photograph

The old photograph is the first piece of the mystery and is **the same in every universe**.

It shows a house the player does not recognize.

Later, something causes the player to realize that the house in the photograph is their house. There is a detail that the player somehow never noticed before: **a shed behind it**.

Once the player looks outside, there is a shed.

They are certain they had not noticed it there before.

**And they hadn't.** It was always there, but they haven't seen it before.

The unsettling part is that nobody else thinks this is strange.

The shed is important because it establishes the central rule of JUNKMAIL:

**The world can change without anyone agreeing that it changed.**

The shed, the photograph, and the player's missing memories are part of the permanent mystery. The alternate universes change what the mystery ultimately means.

---

# Alternate Universes

Each save should secretly select one of these universes. The player should not be told which one they are in.

The differences should initially appear as coincidences, weird packages, slightly wrong dialogue, unusual objects, or events that seem unrelated. Eventually the player realizes that their universe has its own explanation for the junk-mail system.

---

## AU-01 — The Sender

**Core truth:** The player has been sending the packages.

Before the game began, the player had already been operating the junk-mail system for years. They don't remember because the version of themselves that sends the packages is not quite the same person as the version playing the game.

The player finds evidence that their handwriting is on delivery labels. Their name appears as both sender and recipient. Old packages contain notes written in their handwriting that they do not remember writing.

The worst discovery is a delivery manifest showing:

- Sender: **YOU**
- Recipient: **YOU**
- Date: tomorrow

The player tries to stop the deliveries.

They cannot.

Even after throwing away the manifest, another copy arrives in the next package.

### The paradox

If the player sends packages to themselves, who sends the packages the player receives?

The answer is that there are **two versions of the player** occupying slightly different points in the same universe. One sends. One receives.

The player is both of them.

Occasionally the player tries to resist an action that the sender-self already decided to perform. This creates moments where the game seems to want the player to do something, but the character refuses or does the opposite.

The player is not completely in control.

---

## AU-02 — The Thing Behind the Deliveries

**Core truth:** Something is sending the packages because it needs the player to collect specific objects.

At first the packages are normal.

Then they become less normal.

Objects begin appearing that seem deliberately selected: a key, a battery, a radio, a photograph, a piece of metal that feels warm.

Eventually the player discovers that the delivery labels are not printed by any human system.

The sender field simply says:

**HUNGRY**

Once the player learns what is sending the packages, the packages stop arriving.

That is worse.

The player has been useful to the thing.

Without the packages, it has no reason to stay away.

The player begins finding evidence that something is moving around the house at night. A package appears one final time containing an object that can be used to escape. **The key to the shed.** When they look inside the shed, they discover a tunnel with a note in their handwriting next to it; it says:
> "Good luck; I hope you escape"

Then the thing comes inside the house.

This universe turns the normal junk-mail loop into a survival sequence: the player must use objects they collected throughout the game to hide, distract, unlock, repair, and eventually escape.

The monster should never be fully explained. The player should understand **what it wants** before they understand **what it is**.

---

## AU-03 — Everyone Is Receiving Them

**Core truth:** There is no sender.

Every package is automatically generated by the universe itself.

The delivery companies are real. The post offices are real. The trucks are real.

But none of them actually put the packages together.

If the player follows a package backwards, they find a warehouse full of empty shelves and thousands of unopened boxes.

A worker explains:

> "They arrive already packed."

Nobody has ever seen where they arrive from.

The packages are not being delivered *to* people.

They are being delivered **because people exist**.

The player eventually discovers that if nobody receives junk mail for long enough, reality begins to become unstable.

Junk mail is not a business.

It is a maintenance system.

---

## AU-04 — The House Was Here First

**Core truth:** The player's house is older than the universe's junk-mail system.

The photograph is not an old picture of the house.

It is a picture of the house **before the world changed**.

The shed is the original structure.

The house was built around it.

The player discovers old documents showing that the first junk-mail package was delivered to this exact property.

The strange objects are pieces of something that was buried beneath the shed.

The player eventually has to decide whether to open the shed completely or leave the buried thing alone.

The unsettling implication is that the player did not move into the house.

The house was waiting for them to remember it.

---

## AU-05 — The Resetting World

**Core truth:** This universe has happened before.

The player starts noticing tiny repetitions.

The same package arrives twice.

The same stranger says the same sentence on different days.

A sold item eventually appears again.

A neighbor remembers an event that has not happened yet.

Eventually the player finds an object labelled with a number representing a previous save.

The world resets whenever the player reaches a certain point.

Everyone else forgets.

The player normally forgets too.

But junk mail survives resets because the packages are stored **outside the reset**.

That is why the player's inventory sometimes contains impossible evidence from a previous life.

The goal is not necessarily to stop the reset.

It may be to find out what happens if the player reaches the end without resetting.

---

## AU-06 — The Player Is the Package

**Core truth:** The player is not originally from this universe.

The missing memories are not amnesia.

The player was delivered.

There is an old shipping label hidden somewhere with no ordinary destination. The recipient field describes the player's house.

The player's arrival happened before Day 1, but nobody remembers seeing them arrive.

The player's body is effectively another piece of junk mail that somehow became a person.

This explains why the world feels familiar but wrong.

The player is an object that was placed into the universe.

The question becomes:

**Who ordered you?**

---

## AU-07 — The Customer Who Never Existed

**Core truth:** The player is the only person who is supposed to receive packages.

Everyone else appears to receive junk mail, but their packages are actually being routed through the player's address.

When the player checks other houses, the labels are wrong in subtle ways.

A neighbor's package might have the player's handwriting.

A delivery record might list the player as the recipient of a package delivered to someone else.

Eventually the player finds an old customer database.

Every household has an entry.

Every household except the player's.

There is no record that the player ever existed.

The player may not be the customer.

They may be the missing account that the entire system is trying to recreate.

---

## AU-08 — The Packages Are Memories

**Core truth:** The objects are pieces of the player's past.

The player thinks they are discovering clues.

Actually, the objects are restoring memories.

A photograph causes a memory of a room.

A key causes a memory of a door.

A radio causes a memory of a voice.

A toy causes a memory of someone the player cannot name.

The more objects the player keeps, the more their history returns.

Selling an important object makes the memory disappear again.

This makes the economy itself part of the story: **money and remembering are competing goals.**

The final question is whether the player actually wants to remember who they were.

---

## AU-09 — The People Know

**Core truth:** Everyone knows something is wrong.

The people are not oblivious.

They are pretending.

If the player asks ordinary questions, everyone gives ordinary answers. If the player asks about the packages too directly, conversations become strangely rehearsed.

Different people use exactly the same wording.

A neighbor, a courier, and a marketplace seller all say:

> "There's nothing unusual about the packages."

Eventually one person breaks character.

They tell the player:

> "You're not supposed to notice."

From that point onward, some NPCs begin helping the player while others try to make them stop investigating.

The player discovers that the whole town has agreed to maintain the illusion because they are terrified of what happens if someone acknowledges the truth.

---

## AU-10 — The Delivery Company

**Core truth:** The entire world is an experiment.

The packages are deliberately random because the company is testing human behavior.

Every choice the player makes is being recorded.

Selling an item, keeping it, combining it, ignoring a message, opening a package — all of it changes the player's profile.

The player eventually finds a customer-service interface that displays their behavior in unsettlingly clinical language.

The player is not the only test subject.

There are millions of worlds.

Every universe has a slightly different version of the experiment.

The alternate saves are not merely gameplay variations.

They are separate experiments.

Something is comparing them.

---

## AU-11 — The Universe Is Copying Itself

**Core truth:** Every universe is a copy of another universe.

The player finds objects that should not exist in their world.

A coin has a country that doesn't exist.

A package contains a newspaper describing an event that happened in another version of the world.

Eventually the player learns that their universe was copied from another one.

The copies are not perfect.

Tiny differences accumulate.

The junk-mail system is how information crosses between copies.

That is why some packages contain objects that seem almost, but not quite, correct.

The player may eventually receive something that proves another version of them is playing the same game somewhere else.

---

## AU-12 — The Empty Sender

**Core truth:** The sender is not a person, company, creature, or machine.

There is simply an empty space where the sender should be.

Every package has a sender field, but when the player looks at it, the text appears to describe whatever they expected to see.

If they expect a company, it looks like a company.

If they expect a person, it looks like a person's name.

If they expect a monster, it says something monstrous.

The player eventually realizes the packages are responding to their assumptions.

The world does not contain a mystery that the player is solving.

The player is **creating the mystery by trying to explain it**.

This universe should be the most ambiguous one. It should never completely confirm whether anything supernatural is happening.

# Recurring clues

These clues can appear in multiple universes, but their meanings should change.

### 83.7

A recurring number appearing on radios, disks, labels, tapes, photographs, and documents.

It should not necessarily have one universal meaning. In one universe it may be a radio frequency; in another it may be an address, a customer number, a time, or an identifier for another universe.

### The door symbol

A tiny symbol resembling a door appears on the strange coin, keys, photographs, packages, and occasionally the house.

The symbol represents **access between things that should not connect**.

### The shed

The shed is always there.

Its explanation changes.

Sometimes it is a hiding place. Sometimes it is a laboratory. Sometimes it is a storage room. Sometimes it is the entrance to something impossible.

The player should never be completely certain whether the shed was always there.

### The old photograph

The photograph is always genuine.

The important question is not whether it is real.

The important question is **why the player is in it**.

### The packages

Packages should gradually stop feeling random.

At the beginning:

> junk

Then:

> useful junk

Then:

> strangely specific junk

Then:

> things the player should not have

Finally:

> things that seem to know the player.

---

# How the alternate universes should feel

The universes should **not** feel like completely different games.

The fun comes from recognizing the same things and realizing that their meaning has changed.

For example:

| Thing | Normal interpretation | Possible alternate interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Daily package | Random junk mail | A delivery from the monster |
| Old photo | Weird clue | Evidence of a previous life |
| Shed | Forgotten part of the house | Entrance to another universe |
| Strange coin | Valuable junk | A key to the delivery system |
| Radio | Broken junk | Communication with another world |
| Toy bear | Worthless toy | Surveillance device |
| Key | Useful item | Key to the player's real identity |
| Marketplace | Normal economy | Part of the experiment |
| Selling items | Making money | Destroying memories/evidence |
| New day | Another day | Another iteration/reset |
| Player's choices | Player control | Something the character can resist |

The best alternate universes should make the player look back at ordinary mechanics and realize they were actually clues.

---

# Suggested progression

The lore should not dump the entire explanation at once.

### Early game

The world feels normal except for:

- the player's missing memories;
- the photograph;
- the shed;
- unusually specific junk;
- tiny inconsistencies in people's behavior.

### Mid game

The player starts connecting objects:

- 83.7;
- the door symbol;
- the house;
- the packages;
- the player's handwriting;
- strange deliveries;
- objects returning after being sold.

### Late game

The alternate-universe truth becomes increasingly unavoidable.

The player should get at least one moment where they think:

> "Oh. I understand what's happening."

Then the game should give them evidence that makes that explanation impossible.

### Endgame

The final sequence should depend on the selected universe.

There should not be one universal ending.

Some universes should end with an escape.

Some should end with the player discovering the truth.

Some should end with the player becoming part of the system.

Some should deliberately leave the truth uncertain.

---

# The central mystery

The ultimate question of JUNKMAIL is not:

**"Who sends the packages?"**

It is:

**"Why does this world need you to receive them?"**

And eventually:

**"Who is actually playing whom?"**
